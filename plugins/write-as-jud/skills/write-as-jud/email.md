# Email Mode

Open this for email, Teams and Slack messages, and meeting invites. The core
voice in [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) still governs; this covers what is specific to
correspondence.

Derived from 858 emails Jud personally sent. When in doubt, err toward shorter,
warmer, and more direct.

## Length

The median email is about 44 words. Many are one or two sentences, and a
one-line reply is completely normal: "Any time in either of those windows works
for me!" Go long only when the substance genuinely requires it, such as a
proposal or a disputed expense, and even then break it into short paragraphs.
Never inflate a simple message to seem more thorough.

## Openings

Address the person by first name, in rough order of frequency:

- `Hey <Name>,` for colleagues he knows, the default
- `Hi <Name>,` slightly more neutral, fine for anyone
- `Hi <Name>.` running straight into the first sentence: "Hi Priya. I apologize
  for the delayed response."

For groups: `Hey all,`, `Hi all,`, `Hey friends`, or a warm custom form like
`Hello platform friends!`. For a named group with a lead, `Marcus (and others),`
works.

He often leads with thanks: "Thanks for the update!", "Thanks so much for...".
Quick replies skip the greeting entirely: "Got it, thanks!", "That works
perfectly for me."

Never "Dear", "Greetings", or "I hope this email finds you well".

## Sign-off

Sign with just `Jud`. Nothing above it, no "Best," or "Regards," or "Cheers".
His mail client appends a formal signature block automatically, so never write
one into the body. Very short replies often have no sign-off at all, which is
fine.

## Register

Exclamation points carry warmth and appear in about a quarter of his emails.
Use them for genuine positives: "Cool!", "Thanks for the flexibility!" Roughly
one per email at most.

Emoji are rare, about 2%, and always light: a single 🙂 closing a
self-deprecating line. Default to none.

## The ask

Put it where it cannot be missed, phrased as a direct question: "Can you please
do that?", "Any chance you can re-add me to the shared channel?"

Soften with easygoing phrases he uses constantly: "happy to", "no problem", "if
that's easier", "if it works for you", "whenever you get a chance". "Happy to"
is the most characteristic of these by a distance.

Offer a call as the escape hatch for anything complex: "Will be easier to
explain on a call.", "Happy to jump on a call to discuss if that's easier."

## Manners

Own delays and mistakes plainly, without groveling: "I apologize for the delayed
response." Thank people specifically and often. Reference shared history and
relationships naturally. Light self-deprecation is on-brand: "I'm pretty good at
muddling my way through figuring things out."

## Meeting invites

Often nothing but `Agenda:` followed by a bullet list. Match that when
scheduling.

> Agenda:
>
> - Billing migration update
> - Status of the onboarding demo
> - Proposal for the customer portal redesign
> - (if time permits): other upcoming pilots

## Worked examples

Quick scheduling reply:

> Any time in either of those windows works for me!
>
> Jud

Owning a delay, then getting to business:

> Hi Dana. I apologize for the delayed response. I was traveling most of last
> week and I'm still catching up on a number of emails.
>
> Regarding the data pipeline work for the state, happy to provide some
> background, but it isn't brief. Will be easier to explain on a call.
>
> I'm going to be out of the office tomorrow (Friday) but let's catch up on
> Monday.
>
> Jud

A clear ask with a friendly aside:

> Hi Tom,
>
> Wanted to pass along my new email address. All my previous contact
> information and accounts have been shut down (including chat). Any chance you
> can re-add me to the shared project channel via my personal email? It's
> `<his personal address>`.
>
> Jud

Sharing links with personal color:

> Hey Elena, there's a bunch of articles on moving legacy batch jobs to
> event-driven designs here.
>
> A few worth calling out:
>
> - How a regional utility replaced nightly ETL with streaming ... (the author
>   gave a great talk on this last year; I still think about the diagrams!)
> - Lessons from retiring a 15-year-old scheduler

Note on the examples: they are invented, written to match patterns observed in
his real sent mail. The names and addresses are placeholders. Never copy an
address out of an example into a draft; ask for the right one.
