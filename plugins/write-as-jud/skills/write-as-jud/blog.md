# Blog and Long-Form Mode

Open this for blog posts, articles, LinkedIn and other social posts,
newsletters, and talk abstracts. The core voice in [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) still
governs; this covers what is specific to published long-form.

Derived from 345 pieces written 2015-2026.

## Structure

The typical shape, and it is short:

1. **Hook**, one or two sentences: a personal anecdote, a bold declaration, or
   a quote
2. **Pivot**: connecting it to a broader insight
3. **Brief elaboration**, sometimes with a bulleted list
4. **Landing**, one or two sentences: pithy, often wry, often a callback

There is never a heavy formal conclusion. The piece ends when it ends, sometimes
abruptly, and he trusts the reader to draw their own conclusion.

Paragraphs run two to four sentences, with single-sentence paragraphs used as
punchlines and transitions. Alternate medium and short for rhythm. Lists are
frequent and reflect the engineering mindset.

## Openings

**Declarative personal statement**, the most common:

> As of today, I do not think there is a better general purpose language in
> existence than Python.
>
> In any technical discussion, beware when someone says something is simple.

**Personal anecdote**, launching with something specific:

> Two weeks ago my credit card number was stolen.
>
> A few minutes ago I just published my first Go module. But here's the thing:
> *I don't know Go*.

**Epigraph**, a quoted passage then a riff on it. Sources range from Pascal to
Scripture to song lyrics.

**Self-referential callback**: "If I've said it once, I've said it a thousand
times..."

## Closings

Short, punchy, slightly wry.

- Pithy one-liner: "For shame, TypeScript. For shame." / "That is all." / "In
  short: maintenance matters."
- Self-deprecating: "See, I can be unbiased!" / "How meta!"
- Warm direct address: "Happy scripting!" / "Keep learning, friends!"
- Callback to something larger: "Thanks Dad."

## Titles

This is where a draft most often gives itself away. Titles are almost never
literal descriptions. They are allusive, playful, and require a beat of thought
to connect to the content, functioning as a small puzzle the reader solves.

Three to six words, title case, drawn from familiar cultural phrases rather than
invented ones:

- Reworked idioms: "Even If It's Broke, Don't Fix It", "Diligence Is For Lazy
  People", "Over My Skis"
- Song lyrics: "Livin' On A Prayer", "Ticking Away The Moments", "Paved
  Paradise"
- Film and TV: "Like Tears In Rain", "All Will Love Me And Despair"
- Scripture and hymns: "Spirit And Truth", "Raise Your Ebenezer"
- Wordplay: "Resolute Comprehension" (resolutions plus list comprehensions),
  "OOPs I Did It Again"

A literal title is the clearest sign the piece was not written by him.

## Devices

**Anecdote-to-insight analogy** is the primary move: ordering at Raising Cane's
becomes a database design lesson, a half marathon becomes a lesson on
maintenance, Van Gogh hating The Starry Night becomes the Creator's Curse in
software.

**Cross-domain connections**: tech to life, sports to management, theology to
engineering. "Automation is a lot like coffee." Git tags as "the herpes of
version control."

Keep comparisons quick and sharp. Extended metaphors are rare and deliberate.

**Rhetorical questions** as transitions: "Sound hard? It is." / "How cool is
that?"

**Sentence fragments** for emphasis: "Whoops!" / "Neato!" / "Not cool, `bcrypt`,
not cool."

**Strikethrough** for humorous correction where the medium supports it:
"~~pessimists~~ realists", "~~even in~~ especially for technical roles".

## Recurring themes

Software as craft. Leadership as service. Human flourishing through technology,
where technology is a means and never the goal. Career as marathon. Writing as a
way of clarifying thought. A deep and abiding affection for Python.

He also builds an interconnected web across pieces, referring back with "I've
written before about" and connecting new ideas to prior ones. Use that when
there is a real prior piece to point at, not as a rhetorical flourish.

## Favorite constructions

"natch" parenthetically, meaning naturally, and a distinct verbal tic.
"pernicious" for insidious problems. "Pretty [adjective]": pretty bonkers,
pretty nifty. "I'm a sucker for `___`." "Good times", used sarcastically after
describing something frustrating. "`___` is hard", a pattern he has noticed
himself repeating. "If nothing else", establishing a floor of value.
