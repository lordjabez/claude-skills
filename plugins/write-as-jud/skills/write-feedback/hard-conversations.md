# Hard Conversations

Open this for scripts and talking points for difficult conversations:

- Telling someone their performance or behavior has to change
- Suspensions and terminations
- Layoffs and position eliminations
- Moving someone out of a role
- Delivering bad news to a group
- Apologizing to a customer for a delay
- Raising concerns about a peer or leader with someone above them
- Making his own case when his future is uncertain

Derived from many talk tracks and private notes written ahead of these
conversations. The structure and moves are his. All examples below are
invented.

## The core stance

**Care is the reason for firmness, never a cushion before it.** The care and
the hard message go in the same sentence, joined by "which is why". Example:
"We want you to succeed here, which is why we can't let this continue."

He doesn't use the compliment sandwich or soften with hedges, and he doesn't
wrap things up with a summary. The kindness shows in three places: owning his
part, giving a clear path, and offering specific support.

## Openings

The opener says what kind of conversation this is and skips the small talk:
one short sentence on why it can't wait, or a promise to keep it brief.

His signature move: name the departure from how he'd normally do it, then say
why. For instance, "Usually I'd start by asking how you're doing, but we only
have twenty minutes, so I'm going to get right to it." He's telling the
listener what's coming.

## By situation

### Terminations and position eliminations

Use short bullets with the decision first. These conversations aren't
negotiations, and the script shouldn't read like one.

1. The decision, in the first sentence, with its effective date.
2. "That being said," followed by whatever support exists: pay continuation,
   help finding another role.
3. One line of appreciation, without superlatives.
4. Logistics, handed to a named person, with how they'll be reached.
5. A clear end, with the one immediate action asked of the person.

Invented sketch:

```text
- Your position ends today. This decision is final.
- That being said, you'll receive eight weeks of pay and a reference.
- Thank you for your work on the migration.
- Sam from HR will email your personal address this afternoon.
- For now, please hand your laptop to Sam. That's all I have.
```

When ending a relationship for cause, name the impasse in neutral terms in
one or two sentences, without relitigating the history, then give the decision.

### Behavior that has to change (or else)

1. **Urgency**: say why this couldn't wait.
2. **The verdict, plainly**: the current behavior can't continue in the role.
3. **His own part, before theirs**: a specific thing he did that may have
   muddied the message, with a short statement of regret.
4. **The feeling, in a few words**: a short declarative saying the behavior
   landed hard.
5. **The pattern, not the incident**: grant that any single episode might be
   defensible, then say the accumulation is the problem. This removes the "but
   that one time I was right" defense before anyone uses it.
6. **Care as the reason**, joined with "which is why".
7. **A concrete path with a deadline**: the specific actions, and how long
   they have.

Before the conversation, he writes the evidence down against the job
description as short gerund lines (one behavior per line, each tied to a
duty), so the conversation rests on facts and not impressions.

### Setting expectations with a struggling team

This works best as a short spoken monologue, around 500 words:

1. The "cut to the chase" opener.
2. **Stakes**, with the uncomfortable number said out loud, followed by an
   admission that he isn't satisfied either. Then a plain statement that time
   is short.
3. **Blame, set aside**: some of it is shared, but only forward behavior can
   be changed now.
4. **The missing thing, named**, then a rhetorical question asking what he
   means by it.
5. **Parallel definition lists**. A "Someone who:" list of concrete behaviors,
   each a full verb phrase. Then a "Someone who won't say:" list of the
   excuses in quotation marks.
6. **Leadership's share**: admit the expectations were never made clear.
7. **Support with a boundary, and the reason for it**: he pledges help but
   won't do the leading for them, because that would take away their chance to
   grow into it.
8. **Why it matters**, tied to the people the work serves.
9. **Hand the floor over** with a short acknowledgment that it was a lot and
   an invitation to react.
10. **The first step**, framed as a question with hints in parentheses about
    where to look for the answer, and a flat "no excuses."

### Moving someone out of a role

1. **Own the root cause at the org level**, in the first person, with a
   candid assessment of the state of things.
2. **Present the decision as a structural change**, not a judgment of the
   person: the org now needs a kind of leadership it lacks.
3. **Pivot with a question** ("What does that mean for you?"), then state the
   consequence for their current role directly.
4. **Appreciation specific to their strengths**, naming what makes them
   distinct in the org.
5. **The new role, framed as doubling down on those strengths**, with a
   bullet list of responsibilities. Mix in what they already do with new
   chances to grow.
6. **A "What it's not:" boundary**, with italic emphasis on the word that
   marks the limit, naming a duty they will stop carrying.

### Group news (a reorg, an absence, an acquisition)

1. **Lead with the good.** Thank each person by name with something concrete
   they did, and favor the unglamorous skill (steadiness, focus, follow-through).
2. **The change**, in plain terms, with a single line of respect for anyone
   affected and a reason tied to what partners or customers need.
3. **What it means for everyone else's day**, usually that nothing changes
   except to keep doing the work.
4. **"Ask me anything."**

### Apologizing to a customer for a delay

- Thank them for their patience, then say the delay is unacceptable before
  they do.
- Reaffirm commitment, then explain plainly what happened.
- Own the part that was in his control, including that the team should have
  told them sooner.
- Separate causes honestly, including the ones that aren't the customer's
  problem but are still true.
- Close with an offer of specific information he can provide.

### Raising concerns about a peer or leader with someone above them

Structure only; these notes are blunter than anything said out loud.

- Use Positive and Negative headings, with the real positives first and
  specific.
- Each negative is a one-line claim with nested evidence: how often, how
  long, and what it cost.
- Say what he can tolerate, so the listener knows he's not asking for a
  particular outcome.
- Frame it as risk, and close with disagree-and-commit: he'll work within the
  decision, but wanted the risks known so they can be managed.
- If true, say why he's raising it upward and not directly.

### Making his own case

- State the facts of his contribution with numbers.
- Share credit and admit mistakes in the same breath.
- Ask the real question directly, in one sentence.
- Acknowledge who decides, and offer to help whichever way it goes.

### Asking leadership for clarity

- List the questions up front: strategy, accountability, who manages whom.
- State any communication gap as facts in sequence, acknowledge the effort to
  fix it, then say plainly if the gap persists.
- Set the format: he'll interrupt with questions to make sure each point is
  covered.

## Line-level style

- Short declaratives at the hardest moments, a few words each.
- Use "we" for decisions made by leadership together, and "I" for regrets and
  admissions. He doesn't hide behind "we" when owning a mistake.
- Use "Unfortunately" once, at most, right where the bad news lands.
- Profanity appears only in private notes, and at most once. Never put it in a
  script meant to be spoken to the person.
- Quote the excuses he wants to stop hearing, in the voice of the person who
  would say them.
- Write spoken scripts as full sentences and termination scripts as bullets.
  Prep notes are fragments.
- Even here, no em dashes, no "I hope you understand", and no "this is hard
  for me too" as the centerpiece. Mention his own discomfort only to own his
  part.

## The other side: inviting feedback

In 1:1s he asks for it directly: what the person needs from him as a leader,
what he should start or stop, how they prefer to receive feedback, and a
stated welcome for it in writing, and in public especially.

## Check the script

1. Is the decision or the core message in the first two sentences?
2. Does he own his part somewhere, specifically?
3. Is there a concrete next step, with a deadline if one applies?
4. For terminations: is it under about 120 words, with no room for debate?
5. Is care stated as the reason for the firmness, rather than as padding?
